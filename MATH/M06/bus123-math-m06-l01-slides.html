From a cost increase to a decision

Step 1 of 5 · Confirm the facts

1 · What changed—and what stayed the same?

For one bag soldBeforeNew-cost case
Selling price$80$80 · unchanged
Cost assigned to the sale$48$52 · up $4

For this comparison, assume the bag sold carries the new $52 cost. A new supplier quote does not automatically change the cost of older inventory.

First question: are we analyzing a new purchase, an actual sale, or inventory still on hand?

2 · Calculate profit dollars, then margin

CalculationBeforeNew-cost case
Gross profit = price − cost$80 − $48 = $32$80 − $52 = $28
Gross margin = profit ÷ price$32 ÷ $80 = 40%$28 ÷ $80 = 35%

Each bag now leaves $4 less to cover operating costs and contribute to profit.

Revenue per bag remains $80. Gross profit falls; this example does not establish that sales volume or revenue has fallen.

3 · Name the problem before choosing a response

=(32-28)/32$4 ÷ $32 = 12.5% less gross profit per bag

The margin falls by 5 percentage points, from 40% to 35%. That differs from the 12.5% decline in profit dollars.

Problem: at the same selling price and units sold, the business earns less gross profit. We need to recover profit without losing too many sales or compromising supply.

This is gross profit, before overhead. We have not calculated net profit.

4 · Compare concrete responses

ResponseProfit per bagEvidence needed
Keep the $80 price; cost $52$80 − $52 = $28Can the lower profit support the business?
Test an $84 price; cost $52$84 − $52 = $32Will customers still buy enough bags?
Seek a lower delivered costAt $48 cost: $80 − $48 = $32Actual quote, freight, quality and lead time

$84 restores the original $32 profit per bag, but margin is $32 ÷ $84 = 38.1%. It does not restore the original 40% margin.

The $48 supplier cost is a target to investigate, not an available quote.

5 · Recommend a test, then define the decision rule

Recommendation: test the $84 price on a limited basis and request comparable delivered-cost quotes before committing to a broader change.

Measure: compare units sold and total gross profit over comparable periods: units sold × gross profit per bag.

Decide: expand the price change only if total gross profit improves. If lost sales outweigh the extra $4 per bag, reconsider the price and supplier options.

Supplier rule: switch only if delivered cost improves and quality, availability and lead time meet the business’s needs.

Why conditional? we know the unit economics; we still need demand and supplier evidence. Check overhead separately before concluding that net profit improves.

Three ways to assign costs to a sale

Which purchase costs become COGS (cost of goods sold) when we sell a product?

FIFO

First In, First Out

Oldest purchase → cost sold first
Middle purchase
Newest purchase

Oldest costs go to sales first.

Newer costs remain in inventory.

LIFO

Last In, First Out

Oldest purchase
Middle purchase
Newest purchase → cost sold first

Newest costs go to sales first.

Older costs remain in inventory.

Weighted average

Often shortened to WAC

Oldest purchase ↘
Middle purchase → blend costs
Newest purchase ↗

Blend costs by quantity. Larger purchases count more.

Use the average for sold and unsold units.

Allocation does not rewrite financial reporting

“We allocated store rent to products, so rent becomes inventory cost.”

Internal view: allocate rent to understand department economics.

External view: classify costs under the applicable reporting rules.

Often period expense
